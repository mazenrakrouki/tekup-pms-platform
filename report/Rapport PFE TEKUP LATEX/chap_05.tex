\chapter{Release 2: Project and Operational Management}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
Release~1 left a platform that was secure and could be administered, but that managed no project.
This release opens the operational side. Sprint~3 introduces the project, the central entity of
the whole domain, with its lifecycle and its budget. Sprint~4 gives the project a team and the
workload data every later indicator depends on. Sprint~5 adds agile planning, through a product
backlog and iterations.

By the end of the release, a project can be created, staffed and planned. What the team really
consumed is recorded, and the work still to do is organised into sprints. This is exactly the
input the financial engine of Release~3 needs.

\subsection{Design}
The sprint covers the use cases through which a project comes into existence and changes state:
creating and configuring a project, changing its status, assigning its manager, and archiving it
once completed. All of them are reserved to the director, except archiving which the project
manager may also perform.

\runin{Model of the project and its team}
The \texttt{project} module reproduces the identification sheet of the reference workbook: project
code, client, funder, business model, engagement type (fixed price or time~\&~material), project
currency with its contractual exchange rate, initial budget, sold workload and provisions.

  \caption{Class diagram --- projects and teams}

\runin{The project lifecycle}
A project moves through five states: \texttt{DRAFT}, \texttt{ACTIVE}, \texttt{ON\_HOLD},
\texttt{COMPLETED} and \texttt{CANCELLED}. Not every transition is legal: a completed project cannot
return to draft, and a cancelled project is terminal.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{img/state_project.png}
  \caption{State diagram --- project lifecycle}
  \label{fig:state}
\end{figure}

\runin{Sequence of the ``Create a project'' use case}
Creating a project is the operation that opens the whole domain, and its scenario shows both who
may perform it and what the platform verifies before accepting it: the caller must hold the
\texttt{CREATE\_PROJECT} permission, the project code is normalised and checked for uniqueness,
and the status defaults to \texttt{DRAFT} when none is supplied.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{img/seq_create_project.png}
  \caption{Sequence diagram of the ``Create a project'' use case}
  \label{fig:seq-create-project}
\end{figure}

The authorship of a project is recorded but is deliberately not an association. The
\texttt{createdBy} field stores the caller's e-mail as text, stamped by the auditing layer, rather
than a foreign key to \texttt{User}. Modelling it as a link would suggest a navigable relation the
schema does not have, which is why no ``created by'' association appears in the class diagrams.

\runin{Design decisions}
\textbf{The lifecycle rule belongs to the domain, not to the controller.} The legal successors of a
state are carried by the enumeration itself, through a \texttt{canTransitionTo} operation that each
status answers for itself. The service consults it before any status change and rejects an illegal
transition with \texttt{HTTP 422}. Had the rule been placed in the controller, a future entry point,
an import or a scheduled job could have bypassed it silently.

\textbf{Archiving is a flag, not a state.} An archived project leaves the active lists while
remaining restorable. Modelling it as a sixth state would have made it terminal, and a terminal
state cannot be undone.

\textbf{The effective budget has a single writer.} A project carries an initial budget and, once
amendments exist, a revised budget. Every computation reads the effective budget: the revised one
when defined, the initial one otherwise. Only the amendment writes it. In the original workbook the
budget was editable in several places, and the copies drifted; a single writer makes that failure
structurally impossible.

\subsection{Implementation}
The module carries the project itself and the assignment of the person made responsible for it.


\runin{Project identity and manager assignment}
A project has exactly one active project manager at any time but keeps the history of previous
assignments. Assigning a new manager closes the current assignment rather than overwriting it, so
past workload remains attributable to the person who was responsible at the time. A partial unique
index guarantees that only one assignment is active.

\subsection{Design}
The sprint covers the use cases that feed the platform with the effort data: building the team,
planning the monthly workload, submitting the effort actually consumed, and validating it. The
developer appears here as an actor for the first time, since submitting one's own workload is the
only write operation that role performs.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{img/uc_sprint4.png}
  \caption{Use case diagram of sprint 4}
  \label{fig:uc-s4}

\runin{Sequence of the ``Assign a developer'' use case}
Assignment is protected by two distinct checks: the caller must hold the \texttt{ASSIGN\_DEVELOPER}
permission \emph{and} the target project must fall within their scope. The permission answers
``what may I do''; the scope answers ``on which projects''.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{img/seq_assign_developer.png}
  \caption{Sequence diagram of the ``Assign a developer'' use case}
  \label{fig:seq-assign}
\end{figure}

\runin{Design decisions}
\textbf{Permission and scope are separate concerns.} Conflating them would let a project manager
holding the permission act on a project that is not theirs. Keeping them distinct is what allows the
same role to be granted broadly while its reach stays bounded by assignment.

\textbf{A monthly time series, not a grid of columns.} The workbook stores months as columns, which
is why adding a year means editing the sheet structure. The platform stores one row per project,
resource and month. The same information becomes queryable, aggregatable and extensible without
schema change.

\textbf{A month is normalised to its first day.} Without normalisation, two entries for the same
month recorded on different days would coexist silently and both would feed the indicators. Storing
the first day of the month is what makes a unique index enforceable, and it is that uniqueness the
whole financial engine relies on.

\textbf{A validated period is immutable.} Once the project manager validates a month, it can no
longer be rewritten. A later correction cannot silently change a month on which a monthly review has
already been based. This is the direct answer to the traceability weakness of the Excel process.

\subsection{Implementation}
Two additions: one records who is on the project, the other what they spend on it.


\runin{Team assignment}
A team assignment links a user to a project with a role in the team and a validity period. A
developer may belong to several projects simultaneously. Removing a member closes the assignment
rather than deleting it.

\runin{Planned and actual workload}
The model distinguishes the workload plan, which is forecast, from the actual workload, which was
consumed. A developer submits their own workload on assigned projects only; the project manager then
validates it.

platform refuses the second entry.

\subsection{Design}
The sprint covers the planning of the work itself: creating an iteration, maintaining a prioritised
product backlog, committing items to an iteration, and following their progress on a board.
Maintaining the plan is reserved to the project manager; the director and the developer read it.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{img/uc_sprint5.png}
  \caption{Use case diagram of sprint 5}
  \label{fig:uc-s5}
\end{figure}

\runin{Model of the backlog and its iterations}
A sprint belongs to a project and carries a goal, a start and end date, and a lifecycle
(\texttt{PLANNED}, \texttt{ACTIVE}, \texttt{CLOSED}). A backlog item belongs to a project and
\emph{optionally} to a sprint, and carries a title, a priority, an estimate and a status.

The three relations are not of the same kind, and the difference is the model. The project
\emph{composes} both registers: neither a sprint nor a backlog item means anything outside the
project that created it, and both are destroyed with it. The sprint, on the other hand, only
\emph{commits} items for its period. It does not own them: deleting a sprint returns its items to
the product backlog rather than destroying them, and UML allows an object to be a part in one
composition, not two. Drawing that third link as an aggregation would have claimed a second owner
for the same item, which is why it is a plain association.

The usual way of drawing these three classes is a straight chain, where the project owns its
sprints and each sprint owns its items. Figure~\ref{fig:backlog-models} sets that option beside
the one retained, because the difference is not a matter of taste.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{img/backlog_models.png}
  \caption[Where a backlog item attaches]{Where a backlog item attaches: the chain that
           was rejected, and the model retained}
  \label{fig:backlog-models}
\end{figure}

In the chain on the left an item reaches its project only through its sprint. An item that nobody
has committed yet has no sprint, so it would belong to no project and could not be saved at all.
Keeping it would mean inventing an ``unassigned'' sprint in every project and then hiding that
sprint from every screen, every board and every report, which is a way of saving the diagram
rather than modelling the domain. The model retained attaches the item to the project and lets the
sprint commit it later, which is also the Scrum distinction: the product backlog belongs to the
product, and the sprint backlog is the slice a sprint has taken from it.

The multiplicities on the two compositions read one project to zero or more. The zero is not a
formality: a project created this morning has no sprint and an empty backlog, and the platform
asks for neither before the project can be worked on.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{img/class_agile.png}
  \caption{Class diagram --- agile planning}
  \label{fig:class-agile}
\end{figure}

\runin{Design decisions}
\textbf{The link to a sprint is optional, and that is the model.} An item whose sprint is undefined
is precisely an item in the product backlog: identified and estimated, but not yet committed. Making
the association mandatory would have required a fictitious ``unassigned'' sprint per project, which
carries no meaning and would have to be excluded from every query.

\textbf{Estimates are expressed in man-days, not in story points.} Story points are the more usual
agile unit, but the whole platform already reasons in man-days: the workload plan, the loaded cost
rate and the indicator engine all use them. An estimate in points would have been the only figure in
the system that could not be compared with anything else. Man-days keep the backlog commensurable
with the effort actually recorded.

\textbf{Deleting a sprint returns its items to the product backlog.} An iteration that is abandoned
does not destroy the work identified for it; the items simply become unplanned again, which is the
behaviour Scrum prescribes when a sprint is cancelled. The alternative, refusing deletion while
items remain attached, was implemented first and rejected: besides being awkward, it left the items
referencing a logically deleted sprint through a foreign key that was still valid.

\textbf{An item may never join the iteration of another project.} Assigning a sprint verifies that
the sprint belongs to the same project as the item, and answers \texttt{404} otherwise. Without that
check, a caller could attach an item to a foreign iteration simply by guessing an identifier, and
the relation would cross the project boundary the whole authorization model is built on.

\textbf{The administrator receives neither permission.} \texttt{MANAGE\_AGILE} is granted to the
project manager and \texttt{VIEW\_AGILE} to the director, the project manager and the developer.
The administrator role governs the platform, not the content of projects, and the audit that
rebuilt the authorization matrix deliberately reduced it to that. Granting it here would have
quietly reintroduced what the audit removed.

\subsection{Implementation}
The agile module holds three things, the iterations, the backlog they draw from, and the board the
work is followed on.


\runin{Iterations and the product backlog}
The module exposes two nested resources under a project, one for iterations and one for backlog
items, following the convention already used by governance and missions. Authorization is carried by
the service layer rather than the controller, as elsewhere in the platform, so that no entry point
can reach an unprotected method.

\runin{The board}
The board presents the items of the selected iteration in three columns derived from their status,
alongside a column holding the product backlog. Moving a card calls a single endpoint that
reassigns the iteration and the column together, because on a board those two actions are one
gesture: dragging a card from the backlog into the current sprint changes both at once.

\runin{Who does the work}
An item carries the team member responsible for it, shown on the card so that the distribution of a
sprint is readable without opening anything. The field is deliberately optional: an item can be
committed to a sprint before anyone picks it up, and an item still in the product backlog normally
has no owner at all, so unassigned is a state rather than an omission.

The service refuses an assignee who is not a member of the project team, for the same reason it
refuses an iteration belonging to another project: without that check a guessed identifier would
attach one project's work to somebody who does not work on it. The constraint also keeps the module
consistent with Sprint~4, where the team assignment is what authorises a developer to record
workload on a project at all.

this as a mistake does not match the way the company works.

\section*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}
Release~2 turned a secured shell into a working tool. Projects now follow a lifecycle the platform
enforces. Teams are assigned with a trace of who joined when. Workload is recorded month by month,
and the work still to do is organised into a prioritised backlog and iterations. The financial
engine of the next release needs exactly this data, and the data is now both available and
reliable.

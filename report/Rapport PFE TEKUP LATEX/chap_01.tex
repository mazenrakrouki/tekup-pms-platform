\chapter{Project Framework}

\section*{Introduction}
This first chapter situates the project in its context. It presents the host organization and the
subject assigned by that organization, analyses the project-tracking process currently in place,
which rests on Excel workbooks, and compares it with the tools available on the market. From this
study follow the problem statement, the proposed solution, and the management methodology adopted
to build it.

\section{Project context}

\subsection{The assigned subject}
This work constitutes a Final Year Project submitted for the National Engineering Diploma. It
responds to subject~6 of the ST2i PFE Book 2025--2026, on the \textit{C.DEV} track:
\emph{design and development of a centralised platform for the management and steering of
information technology projects}.

As stated by the company, the platform must centralise all information attached to IT projects,
that is owners, project managers, teams, budgets, specifications, workload in man-days and
expenses. It must offer complete financial monitoring covering costs, forecasts, variances and
gains or losses; operational monitoring covering progress, delays, milestones and payments; and
agile planning through sprints and a backlog. It must finally provide dynamic dashboards showing
the state of the project portfolio, team performance and risks. The stated aim is to optimise
steering, automate the collection of information and improve decision-making.

The technical skills listed for the subject are databases, Java, Spring Boot and Angular, which
correspond exactly to the stack retained in Chapter~3. The project ran from 5~January to
30~August~2026.

This framing matters for a reason specific to the subject: the company that steers IT projects for
its clients is itself the user of the platform. The requirements were therefore not gathered from
hypothetical users but read directly from the instrument the company already uses every day, which
is the object of the study below.

\subsection{Project objectives}
Four objectives were set at the outset.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Replace the dispersion of one workbook per project by a single, shared and reliable source
        of information.
  \item Reproduce the financial model of the existing process faithfully rather than approximate
        it, since that model is the company's real specification.
  \item Partition access to information, so that the financial data of a project is visible only to
        those entitled to it, which a spreadsheet cannot enforce.
  \item Keep the authorization policy configurable, so that a change in the company's rules does not
        require a new version of the software.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis of the reference workbook}
The workbook provided by the company (reference \texttt{F-AFF-13}, version~03) is the true
functional specification of the platform: it contains twenty-one sheets and more than two thousand
formulas that encode the company's real steering logic. Its analysis constituted the preparation
phase of this project, and each PMS module derives directly from one or more of these sheets.

The eleven most representative sheets are presented below, each illustrated by a capture of the
real workbook. These captures are \textbf{redacted}: the client name, the contract reference, the
staff names, the cost rates and the amounts of the internal quote are masked, as this information
is covered by the confidentiality of the host company and of its client. What the redaction
deliberately preserves is everything this analysis actually rests on: the structure of each sheet,
its column headings, its formulas and its business logic.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Project identification sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_identification.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Fiche identification'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-identification}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The identity card of the project: client, contract reference, funder,
business model (alone or in consortium), engagement type (fixed price or time~\&~material), project
manager, start and end dates, contract duration, project currency and initial budget.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It is the entry point of every project review. It fixes the contractual
and financial baseline against which all later indicators are measured.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} The sheet converts the budget from the project currency
(here FCFA) into the reporting currency (TND) using a contractual exchange rate; it computes the
risk provision \texttt{PPR = Budget $\times$ 5~\%}, the penalty provision (PPP), the total cost of
the internal quote and the \emph{sold net margin}
\texttt{(Selling price $-$ Total cost) / Selling price}. It also carries the amendment fields,
which adjust the total budget.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Project management} module: project creation with
code, client, funder, engagement type, multi-currency budget, provisions and sold workload; and the
\emph{Internal quote}, which reproduces this economic baseline.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Cost rate sheet (TCC)}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_tcc.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``TCC'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-tcc}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The daily loaded cost (\emph{Taux de Coût Chargé}) of each resource,
recorded per year.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It converts a human resource into a unit cost. Every cost computation in
the workbook, forecast and actual alike, ultimately multiplies man-days by a value taken from this
sheet.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} The daily rate is derived from the annual loaded cost by
\texttt{TCC$_{jour}$ = (annual loaded cost $\times$ 1.7) / working days per year}, where the
overhead coefficient covers employer charges and the number of working days differs from one year
to the next (22 in 2024, 20 in 2025 in the reference file). A validated historical value must
remain immutable, otherwise past reviews would change retroactively.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Resource and yearly cost rate} module (Sprint~2): one
rate per resource and per year, with the overhead coefficient and the number of working days
externalised as configurable business parameters rather than hard-coded constants.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Planned workload sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_plan_charge.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Co\^ut Pr\'evisionnel'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-plan}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} A resource~$\times$~month matrix giving the man-days planned for each
resource over the life of the project, together with the resulting forecast cost.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It is the workload plan: it expresses how the sold effort is expected to
be consumed over time, and therefore what the project should cost.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} With around four hundred formulas, this sheet computes
the monthly and cumulative totals of planned man-days, the forecast cost
\texttt{$\Sigma$ (planned man-days $\times$ TCC)}, and the \emph{Estimate To Complete}
\texttt{ETC = $\Sigma$ (remaining man-days $\times$ TCC)} as a running remainder.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Workload planning} module (Sprint~4), which stores the
plan as a monthly time series per resource, and feeds the ETC used by the KPI engine.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Actual workload sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_actual.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Co\^ut r\'eel Actualis\'e'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-actual}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The mirror of the previous sheet, filled with the effort actually
consumed: the man-days really booked by each resource, month by month.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It measures reality against the plan. The gap between the two sheets is
what reveals a project drifting.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} It is the densest sheet of the workbook (more than five
hundred formulas). Effort may be captured in hours and normalised into man-days by
\texttt{man-days = hours / 8}. The actual cost follows the same rule as the forecast,
\texttt{$\Sigma$ (consumed man-days $\times$ TCC)}, with monthly and cumulative totals. History
must be preserved: an entry is never deleted.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Actual workload} module (Sprint~4): submission
restricted to assigned projects, validation by the project manager, preserved history, and the
actual cost that feeds the EAC.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Billing progress sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_billing.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Avancement\_Facturation'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-billing}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The billing milestones of the contract, expressed as a percentage of
the total, with their amount, their status and the monthly cumulative invoiced total.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It tracks the revenue side of the project: what has been invoiced, what
has been collected, and what remains to be billed.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} Each milestone amount is computed as
\texttt{contract total $\times$ percentage}; the sheet maintains the invoiced cumulative total, the
remaining amount, and the billing progress
\texttt{invoiced cumulative / contract total}. A structural rule governs the sheet: the sum of
milestone percentages may not exceed 100~\%.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Billing} module (Sprint~6): milestones with
percentage, amount and lifecycle status, payments recorded against a milestone, and the automatic
billing-progress indicator.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Missions sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_missions.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Missions'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-missions}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The business trips carried out for the project, with their cost
components: per diem, plane ticket, accommodation, transport and administrative stamp.

\textbf{Business purpose.} Travel costs are a real charge on the project margin; they must be
attached to the project rather than treated as a general overhead.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} The total cost of a mission is the sum of its
components, with the per diem obtained by multiplying the number of nights by a daily allowance
converted at the applicable exchange rate.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Missions} module (Sprint~6): a mission attached to a
project and an employee, with multi-currency cost components and an automatically computed total.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Project dashboard sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_dashboard.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Dashboard'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-dashboard}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The steering board of a single project: margins, man-days consumed
against sold, key dates and the overall health of the project.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It is the sheet the project manager and the director actually look at
during the monthly review. It answers one question: is this project still profitable?

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} The dashboard does not compute the raw data; it reads
values produced by the other sheets through lookups and presents the margins and progress
indicators. This separation between computation and presentation is precisely the design later
adopted in PMS.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{KPI dashboards} (Sprint~8): a per-project indicator
board, and its portfolio-level counterpart for the director.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Statistics sheet --- KPI evolution}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_statistics.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Statistiques'' sheet (redacted)}
  \label{fig:xl-statistics}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The real computation engine of the workbook: the month-by-month
evolution of the financial indicators of the project.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It transforms the raw data of the other sheets into the earned-value
indicators used to steer the project and to detect drift early.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} This is the most formula-dense sheet of the workbook
(more than six hundred formulas). It computes, per selected month, the production revenue
\texttt{contract total $\times$ EV~\%}, the estimate at completion
\texttt{EAC = actual cost + ETC}, the net margin at EAC \texttt{Budget $-$ EAC}, the accrued income
(FAE) as the difference between production revenue and the invoiced total, and the drift in
man-days between what was sold, consumed and remaining.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{KPI engine} (Sprint~8). This sheet is the direct
origin of the hybrid design retained in PMS: indicators recomputed on read from the live data, plus
a frozen monthly snapshot that preserves the official state of each review. This snapshot is the digital
equivalent of the monthly column of this sheet.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Deliverables sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_deliverables.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Livrables projet'' sheet}
  \label{fig:xl-deliverables}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The contractual deliverables of the project, each identified by a code
(\texttt{L31}, \texttt{L32}, \dots) and tracked month by month as a completion percentage.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It answers the question the client asks at every steering committee: what
has actually been handed over? Because the deliverables are grouped by stage, the sheet also shows
how far each stage has progressed.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} The completion percentages of this sheet feed the
\emph{Delivery} indicator of the dashboard, computed as the ratio of delivered items to planned
items. This is what makes a deliverable register a measurement instrument rather than
documentation: a status that cannot be counted produces no indicator.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} The \emph{Deliverables} register of the governance module
(Sprint~7), whose controlled status is consumed by the delivery rate of the indicator engine.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Glossary of indicators}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_glossary.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Glossaire'' sheet: definition and calculation method of each indicator}
  \label{fig:xl-glossary}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The definition of every indicator the workbook produces: its unit, its
meaning, and above all its calculation method.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It is the sheet that makes the others auditable. Two project managers
reading the same margin figure need to agree on what it measures, and this sheet is where that
agreement is written down.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} It states the formulas the platform had to reproduce
exactly: \texttt{Delivery = delivered / planned $\times$ 100}, \texttt{RAF = remaining workload
$-$ consumed}, \texttt{Drift = sold workload $-$ consumed $-$ RAF},
\texttt{Production revenue = contract total $\times$ EV~\%}, \texttt{FAE = production revenue $-$
invoiced}, \texttt{Actual cost = consumed workload $\times$ TCC}, \texttt{ETC = $\sum$ remaining
workload $\times$ TCC}, \texttt{EAC = ETC + actual cost}. It also defines the TCC itself as the
salary cost plus indirect costs, the latter estimated at around 70~\% of the salary cost.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} This sheet is the specification of the \emph{KPI engine}
(Sprint~8). It is also the reason the earned value is entered by the project manager rather than
derived: the glossary itself describes it as an estimate made from the project plan, not as a
measurement the system can compute.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Parameters sheet}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth,height=0.62\textheight,keepaspectratio]{img/xl_parameters.png}
  \caption{Excel workbook --- ``Paramètres'' sheet: the controlled vocabularies of the process}
  \label{fig:xl-parameters}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What it represents.} The reference data of the workbook: the probability scale of a risk,
its severity scale with the associated thresholds, the treatment options, the risk categories, the
efficiency scale and the asset classes exposed to risk.

\textbf{Business purpose.} It keeps the qualitative vocabulary of the process consistent from one
project review to the next. A risk rated \emph{major} means the same thing on every project because
the meaning is fixed here, together with its quantified threshold.

\textbf{Key information and calculations.} Probability runs from \emph{risk closed} (0~\%) to
\emph{proven} (100~\%) in five steps; severity runs from \emph{minor} to \emph{critical}, each level
carrying both a client-side and a company-side interpretation and a numeric ceiling expressed in
percentage and in value. Treatment is one of reduction, acceptance, avoidance or transfer.

\textbf{Derived PMS functionality.} These vocabularies became the business enumerations of the
platform, constrained in the database rather than left as free text (Sprint~7). The sheet is also
the reason the risk register of PMS carries a probability, an impact and a mitigation plan as
separate controlled fields: the workbook already treated them as three distinct pieces of
information.

\subsection{Existing market solutions}
Before deciding to develop a platform, the question of adopting an existing tool has to be
answered. Four widely deployed solutions were examined. The criteria are not general ones: they are
taken from the workbook analysed above, because a replacement tool is only useful if it can carry
the business logic the workbook already encodes.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering

    \includegraphics[height=0.70cm]{img/logo_jira.png} &
    \includegraphics[height=0.72cm]{img/logo_odoo.png} &

  \caption{The four market solutions examined}
  \label{fig:market-logos}

\textbf{Microsoft Project} is the reference for scheduling. It handles task dependencies, critical
paths and resource levelling well, and it does attach a cost to a resource. That cost, however, is
a rate attached to the resource, not a rate attached to the resource \emph{for a given year}, and
the tool has no notion of a billing milestone expressed as a percentage of a contract, nor of
revenue earned but not yet invoiced. It plans work; it does not steer a contract.

\textbf{Jira} is the reference for agile planning, and its backlog and sprint management are
considerably richer than what this project needed to build. It carries no financial model natively:
costing requires marketplace applications such as Tempo, and even then the model is time-based
rather than contract-based. Its permission schemes are powerful but operate on issues and projects
rather than on individual financial fields, so the requirement that a developer must never see the
margin of the project they work on is awkward to express.

\textbf{Odoo Project} comes closest, because it belongs to a suite that already contains invoicing
and timesheets, so the link between effort and revenue exists. Its cost model nonetheless rests on
a single hourly cost per employee, and its margin computation is Odoo's own. Adopting it would mean
either accepting a financial model different from the one the company uses, or customising a full
ERP, which is a heavier commitment than developing a focused application.

\textbf{monday.com} offers flexible boards, formula columns and attractive dashboards. Its formulas
are evaluated per board and do not compose across boards, which reproduces at a different scale the
very fragility that makes the current workbook unreliable. It has no earned-value model.

The table should be read in both directions. The existing tools are strong exactly where the
workbook is weak, in scheduling and in agile planning, and PMS makes no attempt to compete with

in the financial model that ties a loaded cost rate to a year, a milestone to a percentage of a

That asymmetry is the justification for building rather than buying. The company did not keep the
workbook because it was unaware of these tools; it kept it because none of them models the economics
of a consulting firm working on fixed-price and time-and-material contracts in several currencies.
The purpose of PMS is to carry that model reliably, not to reinvent project scheduling.
\subsection{Critique of the existing system}
While this workbook expresses a proven business logic, its medium, the spreadsheet,
introduces structural limitations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dispersion}: one workbook per project, duplicated and liable to diverge from one
    version to the next, with no single source of truth.
    \item \textbf{Computation reliability}: the dependency on formulas and cross-tab references is
    fragile; the actual file provided contains cells in error (\texttt{\#REF!},
    \texttt{\#VALUE!}).
    \item \textbf{Financial visibility}: the absence of consolidation makes it difficult to read
    actual cost and margin at portfolio level.
    \item \textbf{Access control}: a spreadsheet cannot partition information finely; it cannot, for
    instance, prevent a developer from accessing financial data.
    \item \textbf{Traceability}: the history of modifications and decisions is not reliably
    preserved.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem statement}
The central question is therefore the following: \emph{how can the company be equipped with a
centralised, reliable and secure tool that automates the project financial logic currently carried
by Excel, while remaining configurable enough to absorb the evolution of business rules without a
rewrite?}

Two requirements emerge from the study. The first is fidelity: the platform must preserve the
richness of the existing business model, that is cost rates, workload plan, estimate at completion,
margins and billing, rather than a simplified approximation of it, because that model is what the
company actually uses to decide. The second is configurability: the authorization architecture must
be dynamic and decoupled from roles, so that a change in who may see what is an administrative act
rather than a release.

\section{Proposed solution}
The chosen solution is the development of a centralised web platform, \textbf{PMS}
(\textit{Project Management System}). It provides a single source of information and automates the
operational and financial steering of projects. Its functional objectives are to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item centralise project, resource and team data;
    \item plan workload in man-days and record actual workload;
    \item manage billing milestones, payments, contract amendments and missions;
    \item support agile planning of the work through a product backlog and sprints;
    \item automatically compute financial indicators, that is earned value, estimate at completion,
          margins, billing progress, remaining work and drift;
    \item provide decision-support dashboards tailored to each user profile;
    \item rest on a dynamic ``Role $\rightarrow$ Permission $\rightarrow$ Module'' access control.
\end{itemize}

\section{Project management methodology}

\subsection{The Scrum framework}
Scrum is an agile framework for developing products incrementally. Rather than specifying a system
completely before building it, Scrum organises the work into short iterations called \textbf{sprints},
each producing an increment of the product that can be demonstrated and evaluated. Requirements are
held in an ordered \textbf{product backlog}, from which the items to be built are drawn at the
start of every sprint into a \textbf{sprint backlog}.

The framework rests on empirical process control: rather than predicting the whole system in
advance, it inspects a working increment at regular intervals and adapts the remaining work
accordingly.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{img/scrum_cycle.png}
  \caption{The Scrum cycle}
  \label{fig:scrum-cycle}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Scrum roles}
Scrum defines three accountabilities.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{Product Owner} is responsible for the value of the product. This role orders the
        product backlog, arbitrates priorities and accepts or rejects the increment.
  \item The \textbf{Scrum Master} is responsible for the process. This role ensures the framework is
        understood and applied, and removes the impediments that slow the work.
  \item The \textbf{Development Team} builds the increment. It is self-organising: it decides how to
        turn a backlog item into working software.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scrum ceremonies}
Four events structure a sprint, complemented by the continuous refinement of the backlog.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sprint planning} opens the sprint. The team selects the backlog items it commits to
        and defines the sprint goal.
  \item The \textbf{daily scrum} is a short synchronisation in which the team reviews its progress
        towards the sprint goal and adjusts its plan.
  \item The \textbf{sprint review} presents the increment to the stakeholders and gathers their
        feedback.
  \item The \textbf{sprint retrospective} closes the sprint by examining the process itself and
        identifying an improvement to apply next.
  \item \textbf{Backlog refinement} runs continuously: items are clarified, split and re-ordered so
        that the next sprint can start from a ready backlog.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adaptation to this project}
Scrum assumes a team. This project was carried out by a single student developer, so the framework
was applied in a deliberately reduced form, and it is worth stating plainly which parts were kept
and which were not.

The \textbf{iterative and incremental principle was kept in full}, and it was the right choice for
this domain. The PMS business model is deeply interdependent: the indicator engine consumes
workload, billing, cost rates and the internal quote. Building it in a single pass would have meant
discovering integration errors only at the end. Proceeding by increments allowed each functional
block to be validated against the reference workbook before the next was built on top of it.

The roles were distributed as the situation allowed. The \textbf{industrial supervisor acted as
product owner}, arbitrating priorities and validating each increment against the company's
expectations. The \textbf{academic supervisor} followed the engineering and documentation quality.
The author held the development role.

The ceremonies were adapted rather than performed formally. With one developer there is no daily
synchronisation to hold, and no retrospective in the collective sense. What replaced the sprint
review is more specific to this project and more demanding: \textbf{each increment was validated
against the Excel workbook itself}, by reproducing on the platform a computation the workbook
already performed and comparing the two results. The workbook, being the company's real
specification, served as the acceptance criterion.

Two distinct notions structure the remainder of this report and must not be confused. The
\textbf{engineering phases}, that is business analysis, architecture design, database design and
UML modelling, constitute the upstream study work and are presented in Chapters~2 and~3. The
\textbf{development sprints} describe the progressive construction of the software, each delivering
a functional increment, and are presented in Chapters~4 to~7. The distribution of the backlog across
releases and sprints, together with the schedule, is established in Chapter~2.

\section*{Conclusion}
This chapter presented ST2i and the subject it assigned, then studied the process currently used to
steer projects. The analysis of the reference workbook showed a proven business model
carried by a fragile medium, and the comparison with the tools available on the market showed that
none of them models that economics natively. From this follows the problem statement and the
solution retained: a centralised platform that reproduces the financial model faithfully while
making its authorization policy configurable. The next chapter specifies precisely what the system
must do, for whom, and under which rules.

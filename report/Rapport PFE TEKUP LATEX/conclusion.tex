\chapter*{General Conclusion and Perspectives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Conclusion and Perspectives}
\markboth{General Conclusion}{General Conclusion}

The aim of this final year project was to replace a scattered, spreadsheet-based way of steering
projects with one central platform. That platform had to keep everything the financial model does
and drop what makes it fragile. The aim is met. PMS covers the whole functional scope found during
the analysis of the reference workbook, from dynamic authorization to the earned-value indicator
engine. The work went through ten increments, and each one could be shown on its own. Release~1
delivered the technical foundation, the authentication chain and above all the dynamic
authorization model. Release~2 opened operational steering, with projects, teams and workload.
Release~3 delivered the core business value: billing, missions, governance and the indicator
engine. Release~4 unified the interface, checked the security model, and made the platform
reproducibly deployable through containerization and a continuous integration pipeline.

Three design decisions shaped that result, and in hindsight they are the ones that mattered most.
The first is the \textbf{dynamic authorization model}. A permission level sits between roles and
modules, and the table that links a role to its permissions lives in the database. The security
policy is therefore configurable data and not compiled code, so granting a new right is an
administrative act rather than a release. The second is the \textbf{hybrid indicator engine}.
Instead of choosing between recomputing the indicators on demand and storing them, the platform
does both. Live computation means no dashboard ever shows a stale figure, while a frozen monthly
snapshot keeps the official state of each review. That is what the workbook did well, a live
dashboard with a monthly history, without what made it fragile. The third is the
\textbf{normalisation of the monthly time series}. The spreadsheet used a grid of month columns;
the platform uses one row per project, resource and month. Adding a month stopped being a change
of structure and became a simple insertion. It also made integrity rules expressible: no month can
be duplicated, no entry can land on an unassigned project, and no validated period can be
rewritten in silence.

The hardest part of the work was not the technology. It was reading a real business model out of a
file that was never written as a specification. It then meant deciding between three things: what
the workbook did, what the written specifications said, and what the code could reasonably
guarantee. The work also had to be designed for confidentiality. Choosing to compute commercially
sensitive amounts at read time, rather than storing them, is an engineering decision taken for a
business reason and not a technical preference. Those two demands taught more than any framework
did.

Several avenues remain open beyond the delivered scope, and they follow from choices made during
the work rather than from gaps left in it. The most immediate is the automatic import of time-
tracking data. The workload model was designed to accept a second source beside manual entry, so
that effort recorded in an external tool could be imported and reconciled. The design anticipates
this, but the code does not implement it yet. Next comes reporting and export. The platform
computes and displays the indicators, but it does not yet produce the review documents in the
format the company already circulates. Doing so would remove the last manual step of the monthly
review. Further out lies integration with the wider information system. The business rules already
point towards accounting, human resources and enterprise systems. The code was organised in
modules with exactly that in mind: a module can be extracted behind an interface without
restructuring the rest. Last is the question of scale. The current deployment targets one
company's internal use. A large growth in the number of projects or users would justify revisiting
the modular monolith, a decision that was deliberately taken as reversible rather than permanent.

The platform therefore answers an immediate operational need, and it gives the company a
foundation its project steering can keep growing on.

\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Introduction}
\markboth{General Introduction}{General Introduction}

In a digital services company, profitability is decided project by project. Each engagement sells a
volume of effort at a negotiated price, consumes resources whose cost is known, and is invoiced
according to a contractual schedule. Steering therefore means keeping three quantities under
control at once: the workload sold, the effort actually consumed, and the revenue invoiced. Losing
sight of any one of them means discovering a loss-making project only once it is finished.

The host company steers this equilibrium through an Excel workbook, a ``project review sheet''
of some twenty interconnected tabs and more than two thousand formulas, duplicated for every
project. This workbook is remarkable in what it encodes: loaded cost rates per resource and per
year, monthly workload plans, billing milestones as a percentage of the contract, mission expenses,
governance registers and a complete earned-value indicator engine. It is, in practice, the
company's real specification.

It is also a fragile medium. One file per project means no single source of truth; formulas
spanning tabs break silently, and the file provided for this project indeed contains cells in
error; consolidating the portfolio is impossible without reopening every workbook; and a
spreadsheet cannot prevent a developer from reading the margin of the project they work on.

This final year project addresses that gap through \textbf{PMS} (\textit{Project Management
System}), a centralised web platform that automates the business logic carried by the workbook. Two
requirements shaped it. The first is fidelity: the platform must reproduce the financial model
faithfully (cost rates, workload plan, estimate at completion, margins, billing progress)
rather than a simplified approximation of it. The second is configurability: the authorization
policy must be modifiable as data, without redeploying the application, so that the platform can
absorb the evolution of the company's rules.

The platform was built incrementally, in ten successive sprints grouped into four releases, each
delivering a demonstrable increment of the product. This report follows that construction.

\textbf{Chapter~1} situates the project: the host organization, the analysis of the existing
Excel-based process, including the representative sheets from which each module derives, the
resulting problem statement, and the methodology adopted.

\textbf{Chapter~2} specifies the system: actors and their boundaries, functional and non-functional
requirements, business rules, the dynamic authorization model, the global use case diagram, and the
product backlog and its distribution across the development sprints.

\textbf{Chapter~3} presents the technical design: the architectural style and its justification,
the cross-cutting design decisions, the domain model and the relational schema.

\textbf{Chapters~4 to~7} present the realisation, release by release: the foundation and security
of Release~1, the operational management of Release~2, the financial and governance value of
Release~3, and the consolidation, testing and deployment of Release~4.

A \textbf{general conclusion} assesses the result against the initial objectives and outlines the
perspectives opened by the platform.
